Chemical and Hygiene Liquid Filling

Detergent, hand sanitizer and disinfectant are filled on machines that look like beverage fillers but are specified very differently. The liquid decides the wetted materials, the filling principle and how you control foam.

Why these liquids are not filled like beverages

Four properties drive the specification, and all four are usually present at once:

  • Foam. Surfactant-based detergents foam during filling. Foam gives false fill levels and product loss, and it fouls load cells and flow meters.
  • Corrosivity. Hypochlorite disinfectants attack many metals; acidic descalers attack others. Wetted material selection is the first decision, not the last.
  • Viscosity range. The same factory may fill water-thin alcohol sanitizer and thick gel detergent. One filling principle rarely covers both well.
  • No aseptic requirement, but strict containment. These products are not sterile, so there is no sterile filling zone — but spills, fumes and operator exposure are the real constraints.

Wetted materials decide the machine

This is where most projects fail. A stainless machine that is perfect for beverage water can be the wrong choice for a hypochlorite disinfectant.

  • PP and PVC — good general resistance to detergents and hypochlorite; common for nozzles, piping and tanks on chemical lines.
  • PTFE — used for gaskets and seals where chemical resistance is critical, but it creeps under load, so it is a sealing material rather than a structural one.
  • 316L stainless — fine for many detergents and neutral liquids; not a universal answer for strong oxidisers or high-chloride products.
  • Seals — EPDM, Viton and silicone behave differently against solvents and oxidants. Seal choice should be confirmed against the actual formulation, not the product category.
Always specify the wetted path against the real formulation, including fragrance and preservative components. Two detergents in the same category can need different seal materials.

Foam control in practice

Foam is a filling problem, not only a product problem. Four measures, in order of cost effectiveness:

  1. Fill from the bottom. A nozzle that reaches near the container bottom lets liquid rise without entraining air.
  2. Slow the last part of the fill. A two-speed profile — fast to about 80%, slow to target — cuts foam more than any additive.
  3. Let it settle. A short dwell before capping lets foam collapse; the capper should act on the liquid level, not the foam level.
  4. Adjust the product only if needed. Defoamer helps but changes the formulation and may not be acceptable on the label.

Capping and closure options

The closure usually decides the capping station more than the liquid does:

  • Trigger sprayers — need orientation and press-on force; a standard screw capper will not do it.
  • Flip-top and disc-top caps — common on sanitizer and detergent bottles, applied with a press-on or screw capper depending on the neck.
  • Pumps and dispensers — require a separate orienting and pressing station.
  • Screw caps — the simplest case, but torque still needs to be verified on a tester: too loose leaks, too tight cracks the neck.

Safety and compliance points that affect the machine order

  • Flammable liquids. Alcohol-based sanitizers above the relevant flash point threshold require an explosion-proof or appropriately protected configuration, plus ventilation. This changes the electrical specification of the whole line.
  • Oxidisers. Hypochlorite releases chlorine gas if it contacts acid; separate storage and dedicated piping are required.
  • Operator exposure. Local extraction at the filler and capper, and closed transfer from bulk containers, are usually cheaper than retrofitting them later.
  • Labeling. Hazard labeling and the corresponding artwork need to be settled before the labeling station is specified, because it determines print method and orientation.

Frequently asked questions

Can a beverage filling machine be used for detergent?

Sometimes, but the wetted materials and foam handling usually need changing. Detergent foams heavily and may attack standard seals, so nozzles, seals and the filling profile have to be reviewed against the actual formulation before reuse.

Which filling principle suits hand sanitizer?

Alcohol-based sanitizer is water-thin and low foaming, so a flow-meter or gravity filler works well. The bigger issue is flammability: the electrical and ventilation specification, not the filling principle, usually decides the project.

How do I stop foam from causing short fills?

Fill from the bottom at a controlled rate, use a two-speed profile (fast then slow), and allow a short settling time before capping. These three measures solve most foam-related fill errors without changing the formulation.

Is stainless steel suitable for disinfectant filling?

Not universally. Strong oxidisers such as hypochlorite require non-metallic wetted parts in many cases. The material decision must be based on the specific concentrate and its dilution, not on the general product category.
